Movement vocabulary and relationships

Monter/descendre (go up/go down) and entrer/sortir (go in/go out) are opposites. Tourner à la première sortie and prendre la première sortie (to take the first exit) can describe the same route. Lever means raise or lift in suitable contexts; the source’s rejection of English “lift” as a possible translation was too categorical.

Useful vocabulary includes aller, venir, marcher, descendre, monter, lever, conduire, tourner, traverser, sauter, entrer, sortir, partir, prendre, arriver, continuer, suivre, passer, placer, voyager, manger, gérer, essayer, essuyer, lancer, and appeler. Distinguish appeler (call) from s’appeler (be called): the reflexive pronoun changes the construction.

Present-tense patterns

Subject traverser descendre partir sortir suivre arriver
je traverse descends pars sors suis arrive
tu traverses descends pars sors suis arrives
il / elle traverse descend part sort suit arrive
nous traversons descendons partons sortons suivons arrivons
vous traversez descendez partez sortez suivez arrivez
ils / elles traversent descendent partent sortent suivent arrivent

First-group -er endings are -e, -es, -e, -ons, -ez, -ent. Write ils traversent, not ils traversont. Second-group verbs can be recognized by forms such as finissant and nous finissons, with -iss-. Third-group verbs vary: je/tu often end in s, but that is a pattern in the examples, not a rule for every irregular verb; forms such as je peux are exceptions. Arriver is regular first-group even though it appears with movement verbs.

Distinguish present vous descendez / il descend from future vous descendrez / il descendra. The future’s r can help you hear the difference in these pairs, but an r is not an infallible tense test.

First-group verbs are regular -er verbs except aller. Regular second-group -ir verbs have forms such as finissant / nous finissons; many other -ir verbs belong to the third group. An infinitive’s ending alone does not classify every verb. Additional models:

Subject marcher finir aller
je marche finis vais
tu marches finis vas
il/elle/on marche finit va
nous marchons finissons allons
vous marchez finissez allez
ils/elles marchent finissent vont

Spelling changes preserve pronunciation

Subject lancer voyager gérer essayer essuyer appeler
je lance voyage gère essaie / essaye essuie appelle
tu lances voyages gères essaies / essayes essuies appelles
il / elle lance voyage gère essaie / essaye essuie appelle
nous lançons (We throw / launch) voyageons gérons essayons essuyons appelons
vous lancez voyagez gérez essayez essuyez appelez
ils / elles lancent voyagent gèrent essaient / essayent essuient appellent
  • -cer: add a cedilla before o in nous lançons, preserving /s/.
  • -ger: retain e before o in nous voyageons / nous mangeons (We travel / We eat), preserving the soft /ʒ/ sound.
  • Gérer: é → è in the singular and third-person plural; nous gérons, vous gérez retain é. Do not use future nous gérerons for the present.
  • -yer: essuyer changes y → i except in nous/vous; essayer permits both essaie and essaye series.
  • Appeler doubles l except in nous/vous. Jeter similarly has je jette. This is not a blanket rule for every -eler/-eter verb: other verbs use an è pattern, and spelling recommendations allow additional variants.

Imperative: function, persons and punctuation

Use the imperative for an order, direction, or suggestion. Its three basic persons are tu, nous, vous, but the subject pronoun is not written. Many forms come from the present; a few verbs have special imperative stems. Viens, venons, venez (Let’s come / Come) are the three imperative forms of venir. An imperative may end with an exclamation mark, but it does not need one, and an exclamation mark is not what creates the imperative.

Infinitive tu instruction nous instruction vous instruction
continuer continue continuons continuez
aller va allons allez
prendre prends prenons prenez
suivre suis suivons suivez
rendre rends rendons rendez
sortir sors sortons sortez

For -er verbs, remove the tu form’s final s: mange, tourne, parle, crie, continue. Aller is irregular but gives va. Keep s in prends, suis, rends, sors. The spelling is rends, not rent; sors, not sorts/sortits; tourne, not tournes/tournens. In ordinary present statements, the pronoun and ending return: tu continues, tu vas.

Before attached y/en, a linking s can return: va + y → vas-y, mange + en → manges-en. Hyphenate the pronoun. Va à l’hôpital needs no added s, since à is not an attached y/en pronoun. The rule is not “add s before any vowel.”

Use nous when proposing an action that includes yourself: Prenons l’escalier (Let’s take the stairs), Suivons la piste cyclable au bord de la rivière (Let’s follow the bike path by the river). Use vous for several listeners or one listener respectfully; use tu for one familiar listener. Stay consistent within a route. A phrase meaning “a colleague and I” is nous; “you and your colleagues” is vous when another speaker addresses that group. The source confused these viewpoints in one explanation.

The regular command paradigm tourne, tournons, tournez follows the same persons. Finir gives finis, finissons, finissez.

Directions and complement choices

  • Prenez l’ascenseur / Prends la première rue à droite — take the elevator / the first street on the right.
  • Descendez au sous-sol / au quatrième étage — go down to the basement / fourth floor.
  • Tournez à gauche / à droite au deuxième rond-point — turn left / right at the second roundabout.
  • Suivez les flèches / Suis le corridor jusqu’au tournant — follow the arrows / corridor as far as the turn. A U-shaped corridor can be described as le corridor en U.
  • Continuez jusqu’au premier coin de rue / Continue tout droit et descends l’escalier à gauche — continue to the first street corner / straight ahead, then go down the stairs on the left.
  • Va au bureau / Va à l’infirmerie — go to the office / infirmary. To refer to a person, use Va voir l’infirmière. The source confused infirmière (nurse) with infirmerie (infirmary).
  • Monter l’escalier / la montagne / une tour treats the thing climbed as the direct object. Monter dans la tour emphasizes going up inside it, and monter dans le bus means getting on the bus. Montez dans la tour — Go up inside the tower.

Use an ordinal for a floor: le quinzième étage, not quinze étage. Une indication means an instruction/direction; les flèches are arrows.

Learning questions and pronunciation

C’est quoi l’opposé de descendre ? asks for the opposite; monter is the answer. Use de + entrer → d’entrer in l’opposé d’entrer. Comment on dit “to cross” ? / Comment dit-on “to cross” ? asks how to say something; answer traverser. Comment dit-on “leave somewhere” ? → partir. Est-ce que vous connaissez le verbe suivre ? asks whether someone knows a verb.

The third-person plural ending -ent is silent in these present forms: ils traversent sounds like il traverse. Listen to je gère versus nous gérons. Une cédille is the mark in ç; un accent aigu is é; un accent grave appears in è/à; un accent circonflexe appears in ê/û; un tréma is the pair of dots in ï/ë, often signalling separate vowel pronunciation. Say indication with French vowel sounds, and keep spaces clear between verb and pronoun when writing questions.

Statement, question and command

Salut. Tu vas où ce soir ? is an informal question. Je viens si j’ai le temps means “I’ll come if I have time,” with the present after si. Viens avec nous (Come with us!), Réservez les places (Reserve the seats!), and D’accord, réservez les places alors are commands. The imperative is a mood; it is neither the near future nor the imparfait. Qu’est-ce que nous avons fait la dernière fois ? (What did we do last time?) and On a vu aller, on a vu venir (We saw “aller,” we saw “venir.”) provide passé-composé review rather than imperative examples.

In Canadian French, le clavardage means online text chat. The movement vocabulary is useful for map directions regardless of the map service used. No personal account or actual route is required.
