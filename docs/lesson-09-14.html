Number formation, plural endings and hyphens

For 100 and 1,000, use cent and mille, without un. For a million and a billion, retain un: un million (One million), un milliard. Million is a noun: un million, deux millions, neuf millions neuf cent mille (Nine million nine hundred thousand) (9,900,000). The last expression contains cent; neuf millions neuf mille would instead mean 9,009,000.

Cent takes s only when it is multiplied and is not followed by another numeral: mille quatre cents (1,400), but mille cinq cent quarante (1,540). Merely being the last word does not make cent plural: 100 is cent, not cents. Mille remains unchanged.

Traditional spelling uses soixante-sept, quatre-vingt-huit, and mille neuf cent quatre-vingt-neuf (1989). It leaves spaces in soixante et un and between larger groups. The 1990 recommendations also permit consistent full hyphenation: soixante-et-un, mille-neuf-cent-quatre-vingt-neuf. The source mixed these systems; both systems are valid, but “every two-part number always has a hyphen” is not a complete account of traditional spelling.

Quatre-vingts takes s when final in an ordinary cardinal number, but quatre-vingt-un does not. Before a following noun, an exact million/billion uses de: deux millions de personnes.

Determiners accompany nouns

The traditional term “demonstrative adjective” refers here to a determiner. It agrees with the following noun, not with the person speaking.

Form Use Examples
ce masculine singular before a consonant or aspirated h ce chien; ce bureau-là
cet masculine singular before a vowel or mute h cet arbre; cet édifice; cet endroit-là (This tree)
cette feminine singular cette balle; cette personne; cette note-là (This ball)
ces plural, either gender ces chiens; ces arbres; ces bureaux-là; ces étagères-là (These trees)

Cet is masculine even though it sounds like cette. Une personne remains grammatically feminine when it refers to a man. Un bureau → des bureaux uses a written, normally silent x. Une étagère is a shelf, the place where books are put. Un ciseau is a chisel; des ciseaux ordinarily means scissors.

Pronouns replace nouns

Gender and number Near / this one Far / that one
masculine singular celui-ci celui-là
feminine singular celle-ci celle-là
masculine plural ceux-ci ceux-là
feminine plural celles-ci celles-là

Do not repeat the noun after a replacing pronoun: celle-ci, rather than celle-ci étagère. If you retain the noun, use a determiner: cette étagère-ci. English “this one / that one / these ones / those ones” is a useful cue. A complement can also identify the noun: celle de ma collègue. In comparisons, celle-ci coûte 205 et celle-là coûte 185 (This one costs 205 and that one costs 185) refers back to a feminine noun such as étagère.

The bare forms are celui, celle, ceux, celles, normally with a complement or -ci/-là. Compare Ce livre est court; celui-là est long — This book is short; that one is long; and Ma voiture est petite; celle de ma collègue est grande — My car is small; my colleague’s is large.

Neutral forms and c’est

Ceci, cela, and ça can refer generally to something or to an idea without specifying a masculine or feminine noun. Ça is the ordinary shortened form of cela, common in conversation; ceci/cela are more formal. The conventional contrast is ceci… cela…. On envoie ça à tous les employés de la section means “We are sending that to all the section’s employees.” Aimes-tu ça, travailler au centre-ville ? — Comme ci, comme ça means “Do you like working downtown? — So-so.”

Ça coûte 205 is a neutral price statement. The source recommended celle-ci/celle-là when comparing identified feminine objects; this is a useful precise choice, but ça is not grammatically limited to a situation with exactly one object.

C’est fatigant (It’s tiring) means “It is tiring.” C’est contains the verb est, whereas ça can be the subject of another verb: ça coûte, ça va. The source’s claim that c’ is used “before any verb” is too broad: in this construction ce elides before est. Cela est is possible but often more formal or less natural in ordinary speech.

Quantity, price and the verb’s preposition

Use combien de + noun, even when the noun is plural. Before a vowel, use d’, as in combien d’exemplaires. In this ordinary indefinite quantity construction, do not change de into des.

The full verb expression determines a separate preposition at the beginning of a question:

  • Faire partie de → De combien de groupes faites-vous partie ? (How many groups are you part of?)
  • Appartenir à → À combien de groupes appartenez-vous ?
  • Faire confiance à → À combien de personnes faites-vous confiance ? (How many people do you trust?)
  • Voyager dans → Dans combien de pays avez-vous voyagé ?
  • Avoir besoin de → De combien d’exemplaires de ce formulaire avez-vous besoin ? (How many copies of this form do you need?)
  • Accorder quelque chose à quelqu’un → À combien de candidats accordez-vous une entrevue ? (To how many candidates do you grant an interview?)

Coûter needs no extra preposition for its price complement: Combien coûte ce café ? (How much does this coffee cost?), Combien coûtent ces photocopieuses ? Another conversational price model is À combien sont ces deux sortes de lampes ? The source’s proposed equation “de = how much; par = how many” is not a rule: countability and the whole construction determine the meaning. Par often means per, as in par personne.

When replacing a quantity noun with en, keep the number: J’en ai trois (I have three (of them)). With avoir besoin, a clear model is Il nous en faut quinze (We need fifteen of them) or Nous avons besoin de quinze exemplaires. The source also recorded Nous en avons besoin de quinze / J’en ai besoin de quinze; these are conversational quantity constructions, not a reason to add de after every verb with en. For a standard answer without a possible redundant construction, use Il m’en faut quinze. Compare Il y en a trois with the incorrect basic replacement Il y en a trois chats.

Question patterns and pronunciation

Qu’est-ce que vous avez fait ? asks “What did you do?” Est-ce que vous avez des questions sur la semaine passée ? asks whether someone has questions about the previous week. Check masculine/feminine and singular/plural before choosing a demonstrative.

Both Combien y a-t-il de directions générales dans ce ministère ? (How many general directorates are there in this ministry?) and Il y a combien d’étages dans cet édifice ? are usable, with a different question structure/register. Y a-t-il is the inversion of il y a, with an inserted linking t.

Pronounce the t of cet arbre / cet édifice / cet endroit-là and make the /z/ liaison in ces arbres. Spell the forms C-E-T, C-E-T-T-E, C-E-S. Watch v-i-n-g-t, the written plural s in exemplaires, and the silent x in bureaux. In coûtent, the verb ending -ent is silent: it sounds like coûte, not coûtant. Traditional spelling retains û; couter is also accepted under the spelling recommendations.

Vocabulary and register

Faire le plein means fill up with fuel. La fin de semaine is common Canadian French for weekend; le week-end is widespread in France. Administrative examples use un ministère, une direction générale, un formulaire, un exemplaire, une copie de l’ordre du jour, une photocopieuse, and une entrevue/un entretien. The formal vous forms in the questions suit that setting.

11. True or false: use un before mille for the number 1,000.

False: mille. Similarly, 100 is cent.

12. When does cent take a plural s in an ordinary cardinal number?

When multiplied and not followed by another numeral: deux cents, but deux cent dix. Being final alone is insufficient: cent has no s.

mille quatre cents; mille cinq cent quarante. Rectified hyphenation: mille-quatre-cents; mille-cinq-cent-quarante.
